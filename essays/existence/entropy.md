1. Kinetic Constraints (Statistical & Chemical Physics)
In statistical mechanics and chemistry, the mechanisms that slow down or "brake" entropy production are called kinetic constraints or activation barriers (ΔG
‡
).
A system might have a thermodynamically favored state of higher entropy, but if the path to get there requires passing through an energy hill, the transition rate drops dramatically.
Example: A diamond at room temperature wants to turn into graphite (which has higher entropy), but the kinetic barrier is so high that the transition rate is effectively zero. The barrier acts as a physical brake.

2. Thermodynamic Constraints (Non-Equilibrium Thermodynamics)
In thermodynamics, physical boundaries, fixed volumes, insulated walls, and conservative forces are referred to simply as thermodynamic constraints.
When a constraint is removed (e.g., puncturing a gas balloon or opening the door), entropy production spikes. Maintaining a constraint "brakes" or freezes the system in a constrained state.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. Homeostasis / Negative Entropy Exchange (Biology)
In biological physics, living organisms maintain internal order by continually exporting heat and waste. Erwin Schrödinger famously referred to this in his 1944 book What is Life? as extracting "negative entropy" (negentropy) from the environment.
In modern systems biology and cybernetics, the mechanisms that "brake" internal disorder are called homeostatic control loops or active regulatory mechanisms. They consume metabolic energy to keep internal entropy production (

In information theory and non-equilibrium statistical mechanics (such as stochastic thermodynamics), the mechanisms that limit the spread or loss of information are termed information bottlenecks or control policies.
