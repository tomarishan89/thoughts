# Darśana of Kuṇḍalinī

## Opening

> पद्मोपविष्टां कुण्डलिनीं शुक्लवस्त्रां मनोहराम्
>
> — \sa{sri-sarasvati-stotram} (\sa{rudrayamala})

I think that this description of \sa{sarasvati} deserves an adjective which I find hard to find.

\sa{kundalini} sitting upon a lotus, covered with what I perceive as holy and white, and what I witness, steals my heart.

Of course a number of adjectives stand in the source, but this line takes me somewhere totally different. I think there is a difference between Philosophy and \sa{darsana}. Philosophy has more assertion than \sa{darsana}. \sa{darsana} is closer to landscape. Philosophy is closer to portrait.

## Subjective descriptions

I have a \sa{darsana} of \sa{kundalini}, just as a student may have a \sa{darsana} of a teacher, or a borrower may have a \sa{darsana} of what a lender is going to do to him if he defaults.

Before that, a few other entities need to be discussed to set up a basis.

Lets expound upon the idea of these 3 terms. Lets start with \sa{moksha}

\sa{moksa} ~ मम् उच्च क्षयः




Next on the list is \sa{manas} and \sa{maya}. Before these are discussed, I think it is important to look into \sa{cancala}


### \sa{cancala} is not merely “fickle”

I need to put forward an argument: \sa{cancala} is not always *flickering*, *fickle*, or *spry*. Rather it implies “not stationary.” Whereas \sa{cancala} is an adjective, \sa{cancalata} becomes a noun. More often than not, \sa{cancalata} is taken quite lightly, and so synonymized with words like fickle, flickering, spry, and the like. My argument is that an ocean can be a bit more \sa{cancala} than usual — and there you go: tsunamis. Plural.

On the other hand, what does not being \sa{cancala} mean? Absolute rest — 0 Kelvin, if you like a picture. That is the cessation problem for \sa{manas}.

Since \sa{manas} is not \sa{cancala}, \sa{manas} ceases to exist.

Solution? \sa{maya}.

### The root of \sa{maya}

The strict grammatical root: \sa{ma} or \sa{mang}.

If you open a strict Sanskrit dictionary or a Pāṇinian grammar text, the technical root (dhātu) for \sa{maya} is \sa{ma} or \sa{mang}.

- **Root meaning:** \sa{ma} means “to measure” (मापना), “to limit,” “to give shape,” or “to create/build.”
- **Suffix:** The feminine suffix -या (-yā) is added to this root to turn it into a noun.
- **Grammatical meaning:** मीयते अनया इति माया — “That by which something is measured or limited.”

Also let me bring another example of *ya*:

\sa{ya}: to go, to attain, or to move. (Note: the root here is the long vowel या, not just य.)

Example words: याति (*yāti* — he/she goes) or यात्रा (*yātrā* — journey).

### Measurement, \sa{dharma}, and why \sa{manas} is \sa{cancala}

So \sa{manas} is given a means to measure the extent of its existence. In a way, as soon as \sa{manas} comes into existence, the set of tools \sa{manas} has — termed as \sa{dharma} — includes \sa{maya}: measurement whose \sa{dharma} includes differentiating between two unique entities in terms of value. You cannot apply a comparison operator between two alphabets, but between numbers you can — and that difference in value has significance. Difference may be favorable or unfavorable. Difference may exist between smaller and bigger intervals at the same time. \sa{maya} acts as a tool to evaluate.

Hence \sa{maya} is a reason \sa{manas} is \sa{cancala}. Evaluation tends to impart lower value to stopping — hence to ceasing to exist. That offers a challenge against which the tools of \sa{manas} act, resisting failure to uphold the existence of \sa{manas}; hence \sa{manas} is not let stop completely. It cannot be stationary. Hence \sa{manas} is \sa{cancala}.

“\sa{manas} has \sa{cancalata}” and “\sa{manas} is \sa{cancala}” convey two different meanings, and the latter is preferable. It is not something \sa{manas} *has*; it is something \sa{manas} *is*.

### What is \sa{cancalata} for \sa{manas}?

What is \sa{cancalata} when we are talking about \sa{manas}?

What is \sa{manas}? Answer: \sa{manas} is \sa{cancala}.

Where is \sa{manas}? Inside this body.

Who is \sa{manas}? I am.

I am \sa{cancala}. I have a set of factors acting as tools to resist failure to uphold the extent of my existence. One of these factors is \sa{maya}. Another is perception used to measure (perhaps a subset of \sa{maya} — a measuring perspective).

Look around. My claim is that there does not exist any form of life which does not measure or evaluate.

Just as \sa{maya} keeps \sa{manas} not stationary — hence existent — the mechanism of evaluation cannot be functional without perception and perspective.

With perception and perspective, evaluation renders a value to a scenario, either evident or estimated, differentiates between other scenarios, and hence introduces drive and bias based on the value difference.

The dimensions of this valuation system are what tradition calls the \sa{sadripu} — the six:

\sa{kaam}
\sa{krodha}
\sa{lobha}
\sa{mad}
\sa{moh}
\sa{matsarya}

### The gate — coin

Think about it like the following. The perception from perspective delivers a landscape which gets evaluated. There is a gate, and evaluation always rattles the gate. A system is set up which sends a coin. Whenever the gate rattles — whether you attempted for it or not — the gate sends a coin. This coin, when you receive it, the evaluation renders drive towards the coin. With respect to the gate, the possibility of challenge and the strength in the challenge diminish as long as the coins are received. Gate never gets opened; you get dopamine release; everyone is happy.

If somehow you decide no — I am going to open the gate — you have to fight against the coins you valued so much. If you manage to open the gate despite a surge of coins, you pass through it; henceforth you can probably say that the gate is the gate of \sa{kaam}.

Similarly, there are six gates, at least — one for each of the \sa{sadripu}. They are not a ladder. They need not open in sequence; more than one can rattle at once.

### The gate — Indra’s sabhā

The same pattern is like Indra’s sabhā. When a sādhu’s tapas grows strong enough that the order is threatened, the sabhā rattles. Indra does not debate the sādhu into stopping; he sends an apsarā — enjoyment, beauty, distraction — so that the sādhu enjoys life and the ascent breaks. Sabhā quiet again.

So with the gate: rattle, envoy of enjoyment (the coin), drive toward that envoy, challenge weakened, gate closed. Refuse the surge and pass, and you have named which \sa{sadripu}-gate it was.

### What \sa{manas} cannot do — and what these gates are

What \sa{manas} is not capable of by default is control of the gates. It undergoes the rattle and the coin; it does not own the dispatch. That is why \sa{maya} can keep \sa{manas} from \sa{moksa}: the stay-alive valuation structure runs whether “I” planned it or not.

In this \sa{darsana}, these gates *are* the \sa{kundalini}s. Not a portrait of a single serpent only — the coiled locks of valuation that keep the landscape from resting. Some say such locks sit in the spine; that is one reason the word carries *kuṇḍala*, the coil. I am not settling anatomy here. I am settling what I see: six coils of the \sa{sadripu}, Indra-sabhā in the body of evaluation.

\sa{maya} keeps \sa{manas} from \sa{moksa}. So when the gates are open — not as a sequence of levels, but as the release of this whole enable-structure — \sa{moksa} occurs. The fiercest coin, when the whole structure is at risk, I think is pure panic.

That is my \sa{darsana} of \sa{kundalini}: not an absent object behind the videos, but these gates — the ones that rattle, pay, and try to keep the lotus-seated, white-clad, heart-stealing presence from being passed through. The adjective I still cannot find may simply be that she is seen this way.
